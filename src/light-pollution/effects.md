While the effects of light pollution on astronomical observations are probably the most obvious and well-known, its effects do not stop there. Research has shown that light pollution has a range of wide-reaching consequences on the lives of people, animals, and even plants.

## Effects on Astronomy

The effect of light pollution on astronomical observations is probably the most obvious of its effects, and the first most people recognize. Just as atmospheric scattering of sunlight obscures celestial objects during the day, atmospheric scattering of artificial light obscures celestial objects during the night. In recent years, as light emitting diode (LED) fixtures and street lighting has become much more widely adopted, this aspect of light pollution has become even more severe. Most LEDs used in outdoor lighting applications emit large amounts of blue light, which is scattered by the earth’s atmosphere much more effectively than light of longer wavelengths, resulting in even higher levels of light pollution.

## Effects on People

While they are not as obvious or easily recognized, light pollution also causes several negative effects on the human body.

### Cardiovascular Health and Metabolism

Multiple physiological processes of the human body are timed by a biological clock, called the circadian rhythm. The circadian rhythm has a period of approximately, but not exactly, 24 hours. To synchronize the circadian rhythm with the 24-hour day, the body requires external input in the form of light entering the eyes. Randy J. Nelson of West Virginia University and Souhad Chbeir of Ohio State University conducted a study that showed how disruption of the circadian rhythm by light pollution results in disruption of other physiological processes controlled by the circadian rhythm, such as metabolism. The result of this, as they showed through citing other studies conducted on night shift workers, is an increased likelihood of being overweight or obese (<a href="../works_cited#11">Nelson and Chbeir</a>).

Another effect of light pollution, closely related to that of metabolic disruption, is increased risk of cardiovascular disease. In their study, researchers from the Jagiellonian University Medical College and the Johannes Gutenburg University present evidence that suggests an association between exposure to light pollution and "development of cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, obesity, diabetes, and atherosclerosis, as well as overt cardiovascular disease" (<a href="../works_cited#15">Wojciechowska et al.</a>).

### Mental Health

Exposure to light pollution has also been shown to have effects on peoples’ mental health. A study published in the academic journal *Environmental Sciences Europe* discovered a trend between increasing levels of light pollution and increasing rates of anxiety (<a href="../works_cited#14">Wei et al.</a>). Additionally, in their study, researchers of the Hebei Medical University in China present evidence that suggests an association between exposure to light pollution and increased risk of depression (<a href="../works_cited#10">Li et al.</a>).

## Effects on the Environment

Before the invention of electric lighting, the plants and animals of the earth lived under night skies free of light pollution. The change to the environment that light pollution brings forces many species of plant and animal life to adapt. However, not every species is as hardy and adaptable as another might be.

### Effects on Animal Life

One example of animal species affected by light pollution is the Fall Armyworm moth. Yi Ji and a group of researchers from the Nanjing Agricultural University and the University of Konstanz performed a study on this species of moth, which migrates during the night. They subjected the moths to a variety of simulated skyglow gradients of varying direction, and observed that the orientation and direction of the moths was consistently toward the darker region of the gradient, and potentially in a direction contrary to that of their migration route (<a href="../works_cited#8">Ji et al.</a>).

The Phaedon Cochleariae beetle is another example of animal species affected by light pollution. Sandra Regina Lang and a team of researchers from the Bielefeld University performed a study on this species of beetle, where they subjected the beetles to artificial light of various intensities and durations. Their study revealed that the beetles subjected to the most artificial light exhibited delayed growth and development, increasing mortality and chance of predation. The beetles exposed to the most artificial light also exhibited altered surface chemical profiles, potentially inhibiting communication among individuals (<a href="../works_cited#9">Lang et al.</a>).

Anther example of animal species affected by light pollution is the Passer domesticus (house sparrow). DiDiAlice Coker and a group of researchers from the University of Nevada, Reno performed a study on this species of sparrow, observing their activity and behavior under three levels of intensity of light pollution. They observed that the sparrows exposed to the highest level of artificial light exhibited increased daily activity, increased activity and restlessness at night, as well as lower rates of feeding offspring (<a href="../works_cited#4">Coker et al.</a>).

### Effects on Plant Life

Plants rely on photosynthesis, a process driven by light, to generate their own food, so it is unsurprising that plants are also affected by light pollution.

One example of plant species affected by light pollution is eggplant (Solanum melongena). N. Agrawal and a small group of researchers from the University of Delhi performed a study on this plant species, observing their growth and development under conditions of natural sunlight followed by complete darkness, and conditions of natural sunlight followed by artificial lighting. They discovered that the plants which had grown under conditions receiving artificial light at night possessed significantly altered structure on both the macroscopic and microscopic level. Plants receiving artificial light at night exhibited significant growth of roots, shoots, and leaves, compared to plants not receiving artificial light at night. However, they also were observed to exhibit lower chlorophyll levels and photosynthetic rates, as well as a significant changes in quantities of important minerals. Significant damage to cellular membranes and organelles was also observed (<a href="../works_cited#1">Agrawal et al.</a>).

Other examples of plant species affected by light pollution are soybean and cannabis. Madigan Eckels and Bruce Bugbee of Utah State University performed a study on these plant species. Their findings demonstrated a connection between exposure to artificial light at night and delayed flower growth and increased vertical growth (<a href="../works_cited#6">Eckels and Bugbee</a>).

Another plant species affected by light pollution is Erythrostemon gilliesii. Julieta Belén Bariles and a small team of researchers from the National University of Córdoba in Argentina performed a study on this plant species, where they observed its pollination under various levels of light pollution and tree cover. Their observations showed that plants in an environment of high light pollution received the least pollen, while plants in an environment of low light pollution tended to receive more pollen (<a href="../works_cited#2">Bariles et al.</a>).

## Effects on the Economy

Light pollution is the result of light lost into the night sky. This wasted light results in increased energy usage, which, in turn, results in increased energy costs.
